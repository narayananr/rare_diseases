Seeing 20,000 Genes at Once

An Introduction to PCA

A 45-minute lesson · GTEx gene expression · No matrices required

Welcome. Today we'll meet one of the most useful tools in data science. By the end you'll understand the core idea, see it work on real gene expression data, and write one line of Python that does the whole thing.

Most biology experiments compare groups

  • ● Control vs. ● Treatment
  • ● Healthy vs. ● Disease
  • ● Before drug vs. ● After drug

For each sample we measure how active each gene is.

The question is always the same: do the groups look different?

This is the universal shape of a biology experiment. Today we're going to ask the same question — "do the groups look different in their gene activity?" — but at five different scales: 1 gene, 2 genes, 3 genes, lots of genes, and twenty thousand genes.

Case 1: We measure one gene

Each sample is now one number: how active that one gene is.

Put healthy samples on the left, disease samples on the right.

EASY   The eye does the work.

One gene is the trivial case. You don't need any data science — your eyes do it. Disease samples have higher activity. Done.

Case 2: We measure two genes

Each sample is now two numbers — a dot on a graph.

Gene A across the bottom, Gene B up the side.

Healthy Disease

STILL EASY   We see clusters.

Two genes still works because we have two axes — and our screen is flat. The healthy cluster sits low-left, disease sits high-right. The eye gets it.

The pattern jumps out: genes 1–3 are high in healthy, genes 4–6 are high in disease.

Two big ideas live on this slide. First: a heatmap is just a colored table. Every cell on the right is one number from the left, painted by its value. Red = high, blue = low (you'll see this convention everywhere in biology papers). Second: when samples are grouped sensibly, the eye picks up "blocks" — sets of genes that behave the same way across a group. Here we see two "gene modules": one that turns ON in healthy + OFF in disease, and one that does the opposite. Caveat for the curious student: in real data nobody pre-sorts the rows for you. Finding the right sort order is itself part of the analysis.

~20,000 genes

UNREADABLE
  • You can see that the groups differ — but not which genes drive it.
  • And someone had to sort the rows first. Real data doesn't arrive pre-sorted.

We need a tool that summarizes instead of paints every gene.

Real biology heatmaps usually show 50–500 hand-picked "differentially expressed" genes — not all 20,000. The middle panel is roughly that scale. Even when you can squint and see the pattern, you still don't know which individual genes matter — they're hidden in the bands. This sets up PCA: instead of painting all 20,000 rows, find 2 numbers per sample that capture the differences.

NO PLOT EXISTS

We need a new way to look.

No 20,000-axis screen exists. No human can read a 20,000-column spreadsheet and tell groups apart. This is the wall PCA was invented to climb.

PCA's trick — start by going backwards

Back to the 2-gene picture.

If you had to pick one direction that captures the most spread, which way would you tilt your ruler?

Tilted — along the cloud.

That direction is called PC1 — Principal Component 1.

The word "principal" is the same as in "principal of a school" — main, most important. PC1 is the most important direction in the data. Notice PC1 isn't horizontal or vertical — it doesn't match Gene A or Gene B. It's a smart combination of both.

PC2 — the perpendicular partner

PC2 is at a right angle to PC1, capturing whatever spread is left over.

Together, (PC1, PC2) are smarter axes than (Gene A, Gene B).

Each sample now has just two numbers: its PC1 score and its PC2 score.

Think of laying down two rulers — one along the long axis of the cloud, one across the short axis. Those two rulers describe this data better than "Gene A / Gene B" do.

Now scale the trick up

For 20,000 genes, the computer does the same thing — finds the two directions in 20,000-gene space that spread the samples the most.

We can't picture 20,000 dimensions. But we don't need to — every sample comes out with just a (PC1, PC2) score. Two numbers. A dot on a graph.

20,000 numbers → 2 numbers → one dot.

The math behind this is called Singular Value Decomposition — SVD. You don't need to know how it works to use it; sklearn does it in one line. But know that "SVD" and "PCA" are essentially the same thing under the hood.

The reveal — real data

Group A Group B Group C

Each dot used to be 20,000 numbers. Now it's just 2.

The groups separate — and nobody told the computer which group was which.

Crucial point: the colors were added AFTER. PCA never saw any labels. The groups separated themselves, because the gene activity differences were real. PCA just rotated us into the view where we could see them. In the notebook, our groups will be GTEx tissues — same math, just a different label on each color.

The same idea, everywhere

PCA (and its bigger cousin SVD) is also behind:

  • 📷 Image compression — JPEGs are this idea
  • 🎬 Movie recommendations — directions of "taste"
  • 🧬 Cancer subtype discovery — grouping tumors by gene activity
  • 🧠 Face recognition — "Eigenfaces" from the 1990s
  • 🛰️ Satellite imagery — separating crops, water, cities

One math idea. A hundred uses.

Plant the seed that this is not a niche biology trick — it's a general tool they'll meet again.

Your turn — in the notebook

You'll load real GTEx samples and do all of this in three lines:

from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

coords = PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(gtex_expression)

plt.scatter(coords[:, 0], coords[:, 1], c=tissue_colors)

Then you'll explore:

  1. Run it. See the clusters.
  2. Switch to 3 components — rotate the plot.
  3. A mystery sample shows up. Where does it land? What tissue is it?
Three lines. That's the whole computational workhorse. Everything else is reading the picture.

Recap

  • Biology compares groups: healthy vs disease, control vs treatment.
  • 1 or 2 genes — your eyes work fine.
  • 20,000 genes — no plot exists. We need PCA.
  • PCA finds the directions of biggest spread (PC1, PC2).
  • Every sample becomes 2 numbers → a dot on a graph.

Questions?

Common questions to expect: - "How does the computer find PC1?" — it tries every direction mathematically; the math is called SVD. - "What if 2 PCs aren't enough?" — then we look at PC3, PC4, ... — sometimes data is genuinely high-dimensional. - "What if groups overlap in PC space?" — that means the groups really do look similar in gene activity.
