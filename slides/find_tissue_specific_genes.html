Finding Tissue-Specific Genes

What makes a gene "specific" to a tissue —
and how do we find them all?

A 45-minute lesson · GTEx · For curious high schoolers

Today's lesson has three movements: (1) what is gene expression, (2) how biologists measure it across tissues, and (3) how we use it to connect rare disease genes to the parts of the body they affect.

The puzzle

Every cell in your body has the same DNA. So why do they look — and act — so different?

If the DNA is identical, what makes a heart cell a heart cell?

Stress this: every cell — heart, brain, liver, skin, blood — has the exact same DNA sequence. The DNA you got at conception is faithfully copied into every cell of your body. So the difference can't come from the DNA itself. It has to come from how cells USE it.

The answer: gene expression

Each cell turns on a different subset of genes from the same DNA.

DNA is like a giant cookbook. Heart cells read the "pumping" recipes. Brain cells read the "thinking" recipes. Same book, different pages open.

Same library. Different books open. That's gene expression.

Hit this idea hard: cells don't change their DNA — they change which parts they USE. Of ~20,000 genes, a typical cell uses maybe half of them in any meaningful way, and the specific half varies enormously by cell type. This is also where the word "expression" comes from — a gene "expresses itself" when the cell makes RNA copies of it.

What does "turned on" actually do?

When a gene is expressed, the cell builds the protein it codes for. The protein is what does the actual work.

Real examples — same gene names you'll meet again later:

Gene→ Protein it builds→ What that protein does
MYH7cardiac myosinmakes heart muscle contract
INSinsulincontrols blood sugar
HBBhemoglobincarries oxygen in blood
RHOrhodopsinsenses light in the eye

More RNA → more protein → more job done. That's what "high expression" means.

The central dogma: DNA → RNA → Protein. Proteins are the molecular machines that do the actual work in cells. When biologists say a gene is "highly expressed", they mean the cell is making lots of its RNA, which gets translated into lots of the corresponding protein. Why measure RNA instead of protein? RNA is much easier to count — sequencing technology gives us 20,000 gene counts per sample very cheaply. Measuring protein levels directly is harder and not yet possible at this scale. The examples (MYH7, INS, HBB, RHO) will all come back in the rare-disease section. Plant the names now.

How biologists measure expression

When a gene is "on", the cell makes RNA copies of it.
The more copies, the more "expressed". And we can count them.

For every (sample, gene) pair, we get one number — the expression level.

The numbers shown here are simplified TPM values — that's the standard unit, "transcripts per million." Students don't need the unit; what matters is "bigger number = more expression." Point out the diagonal pattern: MYH7 is highest in heart, NEFM in brain, ALB in liver, ACTN3 in muscle, SFTPC in lung. These are real tissue-specific genes. GAPDH is a famous "housekeeping" gene — every cell needs it to make energy.

How we count RNA — RNA-seq

So far: one gene at a time. To measure thousands of genes, biologists use RNA sequencing ("RNA-seq"):

One RNA-seq experiment gives ~20,000 gene-expression values from a single sample — every gene, all at once.

That's why GTEx could measure every gene in every tissue. Before RNA-seq (older "microarray" methods), you could only check a fixed list of pre-chosen genes.

Sequence once. Count everything.

RNA-seq became the standard around 2010. The process: extract RNA from a sample, convert it to DNA (cDNA), break it into short fragments, sequence each fragment on an Illumina machine (millions of short reads in parallel), then match each read to a gene in the reference genome. The number of reads aligning to a gene = that gene's expression. Key advantage: unbiased. RNA-seq sees every gene, not just ones you predicted in advance. Every modern expression dataset — GTEx, TCGA, ENCODE — was generated with RNA-seq.

The simplest experiment: one gene, two groups

Pick a gene. Measure it in two groups of people:

  • ● Healthy
  • ● Disease

Each sample becomes one number. We just look at the spread.

Same experiment shape works for control vs. treatment, before vs. after drug, young vs. old.

This is the simplest possible expression experiment. The shape — pick a feature, split samples into two groups, look at the distributions — repeats throughout biology and statistics.

Meet GTEx

The Genotype-Tissue Expression project — an NIH public dataset.
Browse it: gtexportal.org

Measures ~20,000 genes across ~50 tissues from ~1,000 donors. Free to download.

A "Rosetta stone" for human tissue biology:

Heart Brain (13 regions) Liver Muscle Lung Kidney
Pancreas Stomach Skin Blood Thyroid Adrenal Ovary / Testis …

For any gene, you can ask: "Where in the body is this gene active?"

GTEx samples come from donors at the time of death — each donor contributes many tissues, all measured the same way. That uniformity is what makes the data so valuable: differences you see between tissues come from biology, not from technical artifacts.

Every tissue has a fingerprint

Same heatmap idea — but now columns are tissues, not patients.

  • GAPDH is high everywhere — every cell needs it to make energy.
This is the central image of the lesson. Each row is a gene, each column is a tissue, color is expression. The diagonal-ish pattern is what we mean by "tissue-specific expression." GAPDH at the bottom is the counter-example — housekeeping genes that every cell uses. In a real GTEx heatmap, with 20,000 genes, the same pattern holds but it's drawn at much larger scale — thousands of genes are tissue-specific.

Do rare-disease genes show tissue specificity in normal bodies?

You've heard the names: MYH7 (cardiomyopathy), DMD (Duchenne muscular dystrophy), HBB (sickle-cell), CFTR (cystic fibrosis)…

MYH7 → Heart DMD → Muscle HBB → Blood CFTR → Lung …~7,000 others

Each one's disease hits a specific tissue. Question:

Do these genes also show tissue-specific expression in healthy tissues — the kind GTEx measures from normal donors?

Hypothesis: if mutations in gene X cause disease in tissue Y, then gene X should be highly active in tissue Y in healthy people too — because that's where the protein actually does its job.

Let's check — pick a heart-disease gene and look at it in GTEx.

This is the hypothesis we're about to test. It's worth pausing on the logic: if a gene is "off" in a tissue, mutating it has no effect there; there's nothing to break. The disease shows up where the gene was ACTIVE and is now broken. Caveats (for the curious student): some genes affect tissues other than where they're expressed — e.g., a brain-expressed gene might cause behavioral symptoms that look like other systems. But as a first pass, "where is it expressed?" is a great clue.

Activity: MYH7

MYH7 = a heart muscle protein gene.
Mutations cause hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — heart muscle thickens abnormally.

Every donor — box + dots

Combine both views: the boxplot summarizes the distribution, the dots show real GTEx donors:

Key: box = middle 50% of donors (Q1–Q3) · line inside = median · whiskers = min & max · dots = individual donor samples (jittered horizontally).

The heart box's minimum sits above every other tissue's maximum.

Real GTEx has 200–1000 samples per tissue. Each box summarizes all of them. Why a boxplot? It compresses a whole distribution — every single donor's measurement — into one shape we can compare across categories. Five numbers per tissue (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) tell the story. Heart's box spans a wide range because GTEx pools Left Ventricle and Atrial Appendage samples, which have different baseline MYH7 levels. Even so, the whole heart box sits FAR above every non-heart box, which are near-invisible smears at the x-axis. The takeaway: when a gene is truly tissue-specific, you don't just see it in the medians — you see it in every single donor.

So… how do we find them computationally?

We just used our eyes to confirm tissue-specific expression for a handful of genes:

MYH7 → Heart DMD → Muscle INS → Pancreas HBB → Blood

That worked for 4 genes. But the human genome has…

~20,000 genes

We can't eyeball 20,000 bar charts. We need a number per gene that says "how tissue-specific is this?" — and apply it everywhere at once.

Your turn — describe the steps

Given a GTEx-style table like the one we just looked at, what steps would you take to find tissue-specific genes?

Write down your plan as a numbered list. Some prompts to guide you:
  • Each tissue has many donors — what do you do with all those numbers?
  • You need to compare one gene's expression across tissues — what would you compare?
  • How would you decide a gene is "specific enough" to keep?
  • If your algorithm gives every gene a score, how do you use the score to make a list?

There's no one right answer. Many real methods exist.

Next lesson: we'll walk through three of the most common ones and see why scientists disagree on which is best.

Expected student answers (collect on the board): Step 1: "Average (or take the median of) the donors for each (gene, tissue)" — collapses many donors into one number per cell. Step 2: "For each gene, look at how the tissue values compare" — but HOW? This is where methods diverge. Step 3: "Pick the top tissue and divide by something" — could be - the second-highest tissue (fold change) - the average tissue (ratio to mean) - a more complex sum (tau) Step 4: "Threshold the score and keep the top genes." Don't tell them the methods yet — let them invent their own. Often students reinvent fold change naturally. Tau is harder to discover.
